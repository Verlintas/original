A license is a permission slip. Without one, other people technically have no right to copy, modify, or redistribute your work, even if it is public on the internet.

## START WITH THE QUESTION

Ask what you want to allow:

- **Do anything, keep it simple:** MIT, ISC, or 0BSD.
- **Do anything, but keep my name:** MIT, BSD-3-Clause, or Apache-2.0.
- **Keep changes to the same files open:** MPL-2.0 or EPL-2.0.
- **Keep the whole project open:** GPL-3.0.
- **Keep network services open:** AGPL-3.0.
- **Give it away completely:** CC0-1.0 or Unlicense, where appropriate.

The [license chooser](/universe/license-chooser/) turns those answers into a shortlist. The [comparison table](/universe/license-compare/) shows the details.

## THREE QUESTIONS THAT MATTER

1. **Do you need an explicit patent grant?** Apache-2.0 and GPL-3.0 include one. MIT does not.
2. **May modified versions stay closed?** If yes, do not use a strong copyleft license.
3. **Is this software, content, or data?** Creative Commons licenses are designed for creative works; they are usually a poor fit for software.

## WHAT TO DO

- Pick one license; do not invent a custom license unless you have a lawyer.
- Put the full text in a `LICENSE` file at the project root.
- Add a short header or SPDX identifier to source files where appropriate.
- Keep the copyright notice.
- Link to the license from the README.

If you change your mind later, you can publish a new version under a new license, but you cannot revoke permissions already granted for old copies.
